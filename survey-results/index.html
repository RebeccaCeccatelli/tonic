Listening study · Results

What you heard

Between 8 and 12 November 2026, 58 people gave up about twenty minutes to listen to short pieces of music, most of them written by computers, and to tell me what they heard. This is what came out of it.

If you were one of them: thank you. Every answer went into the thesis.

What you listened to

Every clip opened with a second or so of a real piece of music. From there, something carried on: either the rest of the real piece, or one of three computer models that had learned from the same large collection of music files.

Tonic

The model from my thesis

It doesn't commit to the next note straight away: it starts from a rough guess and refines it over a few steps. That refining step is also where it can be steered.

Llama & GPT-2

The usual approach

Two well-known designs, the kind behind many chatbots, trained on exactly the same music. They pick each next note in one go.

The original

The real thing

How the piece actually continues, hidden among the others as a reference point. You were never told which one it was.

Two ways to write music down

Computers don't read sheet music, so music first has to become a sequence of symbols. I tried two ways. REMI goes bar by bar and records how hard each note is played. Anticipation lists notes by their timing and has no loudness at all, which is why every Anticipation clip played at the same volume.

Steering

Steering means asking a model, while it writes, for music that's louder or softer, with longer or shorter notes, or higher or lower. Tonic does it inside its refining step. For Llama and GPT-2 I used common techniques: nudging the model's choices toward the target as it writes, or writing many versions and keeping the one that comes closest.

The survey had three parts

ABCD
Part 1

Which sounds most musical?

Four versions of the same opening, in random order: Tonic, Llama, GPT-2 and the original. You picked the most and the least musical.

Plain→Steered
Part 2

Did it change?

The same model twice: once on its own, once steered. You said what you heard, from "clearly softer" through "no difference" to "clearly louder" (or shorter/longer, lower/higher).

ABC
Part 3

Steered, side by side

Two to four models, all steered the same way on the same opening. Again: most and least musical.

After every question there was an optional box: did anything sound cacophonous? Harsh, chaotic, almost random notes. Hidden among the questions were also two checks: a clip steered so hard it's close to noise, and a pair of identical clips.

What came out

Part 1 · Most musical

With REMI, Tonic held its own against the real music

The score is simple: how often a version was picked as most musical, minus how often it was picked as least musical. A version that always won would score +1; one that always lost, −1.

With REMI, Tonic's continuations scored about the same as the originals (+0.17 against +0.20) and clearly above Llama and GPT-2.

With Anticipation the four were close. Llama came out a little ahead, and the originals came last, just behind GPT-2. That's mostly three real pieces: very dense arrangements (low brass, clashing high strings, distorted guitar over a repeated piano note) that many of you picked as least musical. I listened to them again and they play correctly. Real music isn't always pleasant music.

Part 2 · Did it change?

Most of the time, you heard the change, and in the right direction

Across every model and every kind of change, here's how the answers split:

60%right direction
20%opposite
20%no difference

When you did hear a change, the right direction beat the wrong one three to one. Overall Tonic and the other methods were heard equally often (58% and 61%).

Loudness was Tonic's clearest change: 81% of answers got it right. Its weakest was pitch with Anticipation, where most of you heard no difference or the opposite one.

In general, changes were easier to hear with REMI than with Anticipation, whichever model did the steering.

Part 2 · A closer look

Loudness and pitch are easy to hear. Note length is trickier

For every clip I also know how much the music actually changed. For loudness and pitch, your ears followed the numbers: when a steered clip had really moved, about three in four answers heard it move the right way. When it hadn't, most of you didn't hear it move either.

Note length didn't line up. You often heard a change, but how big it sounded had nothing to do with how much my measure of note length changed. That measure averages over every note, drums included, and that isn't what makes music sound short and detached or long and smooth. Something to fix in the future.

Part 3 · Steered, side by side

Steering didn't cost much musicality

When the steered versions of different models were played side by side, none of them clearly won. Tonic sat in the middle with both formats.

The one that did best, by a small margin, was GPT-2 with "write many, keep the closest". That method never changes how the music is written. It just picks one of many ordinary versions. Since it wasn't clearly preferred over the ones steered while writing, steering doesn't seem to make the music much worse.

The optional box · Cacophonous?

Steered Tonic rarely sounded harsh

In Part 2 you heard each model plain and steered, so we can see how often steering made you tick the box. For Tonic it went up by 2 to 6 points; for the nudging methods, by 7 to 14.

Steered Tonic was ticked in 7% of cases with REMI and 6% with Anticipation, the lowest of all models with both formats. The numbers per model are small, so read it as a tendency rather than a ranking.

And the box did its job: 74% of you flagged the clip that was steered into near-noise.

Who listened

Musicians and non-musicians heard the same things

Whether you had no musical training or a music degree made no reliable difference to the answers. Both groups heard the steering in the right direction 59% of the time.

If anything, the listeners with the most training were a bit sharper: 89% of them flagged the near-noise clip, against 61% of those without training, and all of them spotted the identical pair.
