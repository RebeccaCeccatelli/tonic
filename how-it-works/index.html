How Tonic works

How a computer can write music, what Tonic does differently, and how you can steer it while it plays. No maths, and a few things to listen to along the way.

6 short chaptersabout 8 minutesheadphones help
01

Music, spelled out

A computer doesn't hear music. It reads it.

To learn music, a computer first needs it written down as a list of symbols, a bit like letters in a text. Each note becomes a few symbols: where it starts, which instrument plays it, how high it is, how loud, how long it lasts.

Writing music then comes down to one question, asked again and again: what is the next symbol? Answer it a few hundred times and you have a piece.

The highlighted symbols belong to the note lit up on the left (notes that start together share their beat). I used two such "alphabets" in the thesis, called REMI and Anticipation.

02

How most AI models do it

Look at everything so far, pick the next symbol, move on.

Most AI models that write text or music, including the ones behind chatbots, work like a very well-read autocomplete. They look at everything written so far, give each possible next symbol a chance, and pick one in a single go. Then they move on to the next one.

It works remarkably well. But each choice is made at once, with no second look.

Which note comes next?

The model gives every candidate a chance, picks, and that's it. No going back.

03

Tonic thinks twice

Draft, check, improve.

Tonic is a different kind of model, called an Energy-Based Transformer. Instead of picking the next symbol straight away, it starts from a rough draft: a vague idea of what could come next.

It then checks the draft with a single number, its energy. Low energy means "this fits the music so far", high energy means "something's off". Tonic nudges the draft downhill, toward lower energy, a couple of times. Only then does it write the symbol down.

It's a little like tuning a guitar string: you pluck, hear that it's off, turn the peg a bit, pluck again. Tonic doesn't keep going forever, though: two small turns turned out to be enough.
04

Steering with the same energy

Add a wish to the landscape, and the music rolls toward it.

Here's the idea at the heart of the thesis. The energy is just a number Tonic tries to make small, so we can add our own wish to it. A small helper model listens for one musical feature, say loudness, and adds a penalty whenever the draft sounds further from what we asked for.

The landscape tilts. Tonic still looks for the lowest point, but that point now sits where the music is louder (or softer, or higher, or has longer notes). Tonic itself doesn't need to be retrained, and a dial sets how strongly the wish counts.

06

What I found

The good news, and the honest limits.

I compared Tonic with two traditional models (the autocomplete kind from chapter 2), of the same size and trained on exactly the same music, and with the usual ways of steering them.

≈

It writes music about as well as traditional models

With the REMI alphabet, Tonic learned music as well as the traditional models. With the second alphabet it stayed a little behind.

95%

Steering works

With REMI, asked for louder or softer music, Tonic moved the right way in 95 of 100 pieces, as accurately as the best traditional techniques, and without making the music harsh.

People heard it

In a blind listening survey, 58 people heard the requested change in the right direction in 60% of answers, and rated Tonic's best REMI continuations as musical as real music. See the survey results.

6×

But it isn't easier to steer

A traditional technique reaches the same accuracy with six times less computing. Tonic also only works within a narrow range of settings: turn the dial too far and the music falls apart.

2

Thinking longer didn't help

The hope with this kind of model is that more rounds of checking give better music. Here, two rounds were best. Tonic learned to take a short path downhill, and its sense of "fits" only works along that path.

?

Why steering is fragile

The wish acts while Tonic's draft is still vague. Push too hard and the draft stays blurry, so Tonic ends up picking almost at random among, say, all the loud notes.
